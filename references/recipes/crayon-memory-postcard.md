# Crayon Memory Postcard

## Contract

- **Status:** candidate
- **Default mode:** Production
- **Input:** one photograph
- **Output:** one 3:4 photo-and-crayon travel postcard per input
- **Best for:** warm travel, family, animal, street, and everyday-memory photographs
- **Fidelity:** the upper half remains source-faithful; the lower half may simplify
  only what is redrawn from memory

## Creative kernel

Tell one memory twice. Keep the upper half photographic and translate the lower half
into a small crayon scene on warm paper.

Choose only two to five identity-carrying elements from the source. Redraw them with
uneven dark lines, visible wax grain, loose hatching, imperfect fill, and two to four
source-derived colors. The result should feel observant and personal: naive without
being childish, collectible without becoming commercial.

Leave enough paper around the miniature scene for the handmade marks to breathe.
The lower image is a recollection, not a filter applied to the whole photograph.

## Adaptation rules

- Keep one output per input and use a 3:4 vertical canvas.
- Start from equal photographic and illustrated regions.
- Preserve subject count, identity, pose, key objects, perspective, and relationships
  in the photographic region.
- Let the source decide which few elements survive in the crayon scene.
- Use crayon as the dominant medium; colored pencil may add small details.
- Sample two to four recognizable colors from the photo without washing them out.
- Add at most two short handwritten phrases derived from the scene. Leave the text
  area blank when wording or spelling is uncertain.

## Production finishing

For an exact 50:50 layout, normalize the source non-destructively, generate the lower
artwork separately, and assemble both halves on a fixed 3:4 canvas. Verify the source
region against the normalized input.

## Avoid

- polished vector lines or clean digital gradients;
- watercolor, oil paint, 3D, anime, sticker, or commercial-cartoon styling;
- invented people, props, locations, dates, frames, or travel slogans;
- pale gray color that loses the source's memorable accent;
- tape, pins, torn-paper borders, postage motifs, or scrapbook clutter; and
- changing the source subjects to make the composition easier.

## Review questions

- Is the upper half still the supplied photograph?
- Does the lower scene read as a selective memory rather than a traced copy?
- Do wax grain, broken lines, and imperfect fill feel physically handmade?
- Is it charming without becoming a children's product illustration?
