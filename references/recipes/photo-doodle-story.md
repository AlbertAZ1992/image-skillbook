# Photo Doodle Story

## Contract

- **Status:** candidate
- **Default mode:** Production
- **Input:** one photograph with a readable subject, action, or relationship
- **Output:** one 3:4 editorial diptych with a source photo and a playful photo-doodle
  reinterpretation
- **Best for:** people, pets, movement, companionship, and small everyday narratives
- **Fidelity:** source identity remains photographic; drawn characters may interact
  with it but must not replace it

## Creative kernel

Read the photograph for a gesture, relationship, emotion, or small comic possibility.
Keep the upper half as the real photograph. In the lower half, isolate the essential
real subject and let a few tiny black-line figures participate in its story.

The figures should behave like thoughtful marginalia: imperfect, understated, and
responsive to the source action. Use a warm near-white field, restrained source-color
accents, and ample empty space. The humor should arrive through interaction, not
through decorative stickers.

## Adaptation rules

- Use one vertical 3:4 poster per input with equal photo and story regions.
- Keep the original subject's identity, body, clothing, texture, and color intact.
- Select one narrative idea before adding any drawn element.
- Add only the line figures and props needed to express that idea.
- Let doodles observe, guide, follow, climb, carry, imitate, or react to the subject.
- Use thin black or dark-gray lines with natural wobble and no polished vector finish.
- Add at most two short handwritten thoughts; omit them if they only describe objects.

## Production finishing

When possible, use a source-locked cutout for the lower subject and composite it with
the generated doodle field. Assemble the untouched upper region and verify the final
3:4 canvas and equal split deterministically.

## Avoid

- turning the real subject into a cartoon or changing identity, pose, or clothing;
- unrelated doodles, excessive props, scrapbook collage, or infographic layout;
- children's-book, advertising, or polished commercial character styling;
- large headlines, generic inspiration quotes, or dense handwriting; and
- filling negative space merely because it is empty.

## Review questions

- Is the lower subject recognizably the same photographic subject?
- Do the doodles reveal a specific reading of this photograph?
- Is the interaction legible with very few marks?
- Does the piece feel warm and editorial rather than cute for its own sake?
